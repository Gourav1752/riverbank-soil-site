Sections 2, 6 and 7

Soil nutrients and how flooding changes them

A soil that was nutrient-rich before a flood may be deficient in some nutrients afterwards, while others increase because of sediment deposition. This page sets out why.

Section 2 · Introduction

Why riverbank land is vulnerable

Flooding is one of the major natural events capable of changing the physical, chemical and biological properties of soil. Riverbank regions are particularly exposed, because an overflowing river carries large quantities of water, sediment, dissolved minerals and organic matter onto the surrounding agricultural land.

The effect on fertility is not uniform. Some nutrients are removed through leaching, runoff, erosion and other processes; others arrive with nutrient-rich sediment. Prolonged waterlogging also reduces oxygen in the soil and changes the chemical forms of certain nutrients.

Comparing samples taken before and after the flood shows these changes directly. The results guide crop selection, fertilizer application and post-flood soil restoration.

Section 7 · Overview

Losses and gains in the same flood

The final effect depends on flood duration, soil characteristics, water quality, sediment composition, drainage conditions and erosion.

Removed from the root zone

Nutrient loss

  • Leaching: dissolved nutrients carried downward by water
  • Surface runoff: nutrients washed off the field
  • Soil erosion: nutrient-bearing soil carried away
  • Denitrification: nitrogen lost as gas in waterlogged soil
  • Removal of topsoil

Changed in place

Waterlogging

  • Oxygen availability drops
  • Oxidation–reduction (redox) conditions change
  • Iron and manganese change chemical form, which affects their availability
  • Soil pH may shift
  • Phosphorus may temporarily become more available

Added to the soil

Deposition

  • Sediment deposition raises concentrations of some nutrients
  • Organic matter carried in by floodwater
  • Minerals such as calcium and magnesium in deposited sediment
  • Salts, which can raise electrical conductivity

Section 6.1 · Macronutrients

Nutrients needed in large quantities

Macronutrients are required in relatively large quantities and are important indicators of soil fertility.

Small quantities, large effects

Micronutrients are required in much smaller quantities, but a deficiency can still significantly affect plant growth and development.

Sections 7.1–7.5

Nutrient by nutrient

Nitrogen N · 7.1

Nitrogen can be lost through leaching, surface runoff, denitrification and soil erosion. Extended waterlogging may therefore reduce the nitrogen available to plants.

Mechanisms
  • Leaching
  • Runoff
  • Denitrification
  • Erosion

Phosphorus P · 7.2

Flooding can change phosphorus availability depending on soil conditions. In some situations phosphorus temporarily becomes more available, while erosion and sediment movement can carry it away from the original soil.

Mechanisms
  • Temporary availability
  • Erosion
  • Sediment movement

Potassium K · 7.3

Potassium is relatively mobile in soil. Floodwater and erosion can transport it away from the root zone, potentially reducing available potassium.

Mechanisms
  • Mobility in floodwater
  • Erosion

Calcium & magnesium Ca, Mg · 7.4

Calcium and magnesium may be lost through leaching or erosion. Their concentration can also increase if floodwater deposits sediment containing these minerals.

Mechanisms
  • Leaching
  • Erosion
  • Sediment deposition

Iron & manganese Fe, Mn · 7.5

Flooding changes the oxidation–reduction conditions of soil. Under waterlogged conditions iron and manganese change their chemical forms, which may affect their availability to plants.

Mechanisms
  • Redox change
  • Waterlogging
